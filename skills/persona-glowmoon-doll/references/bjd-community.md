## § PART 1 — History

### Ancient roots to 19th century
Articulated dolls trace to ancient Egypt, Greece, and Rome — clay and wood
figures with jointed limbs connected by wire or string. The modern ball-joint
emerged in late 19th-century France and Germany: bisque-headed dolls with
composition bodies (pulp, sawdust, glue) articulated by true ball-and-socket
joints. These are now collectible antiques.

### Hans Bellmer (1930s)
German surrealist artist Hans Bellmer built provocative, highly articulated
dolls in the 1930s and used them as photographic subjects. He introduced
the concept of artful doll photography — the doll as a sculptural object
for artistic image-making. His work influenced Japanese doll artists and is
considered a philosophical ancestor to the modern BJD community.

### Japanese art doll tradition
Inspired by Bellmer and Japan's own rich ningyō (doll) tradition, Japanese
artists developed bisque art dolls — often 60–120cm tall, purely for display
and photography, not collecting. Artists like Koitsukihime (active since the
1980s) command thousands to hundreds of thousands of dollars per piece.
This fine art doll community remains active in Japan and produces regular
artbooks. It is the cultural ancestor of commercial BJDs but a distinct
and much more exclusive category.

### Modern ABJD origin: Volks Super Dollfie (1999)
The commercial modern BJD begins with the Japanese company Volks, which
primarily produced garage kits (anime resin figure kits). In 1999 they
released the Super Dollfie — 57cm, polyurethane resin, elastic-strung,
ball-jointed, and designed for customization. The goal was to reach a
female market for their resin products. Super Dollfie is a registered
trademark; its common use as a generic term for all BJDs is technically
incorrect but ubiquitous.

### Korean expansion (2002–2003)
South Korean companies entered rapidly after Volks. Cerberus Project
(creating the Delf line, manufactured by Luts), Customhouse, and others
established Korea as the dominant production center. Korea remains the
largest BJD manufacturer base today.

### China enters (mid-2000s)
Early Chinese BJDs were often knockoffs of Japanese/Korean designs in
low-quality materials. Dollzone was the first Chinese company to release
original sculpts in high-quality polyurethane resin. China now produces
a wide range of BJDs across all quality tiers.

### Western indie artist wave (2010s–present)
Artists in the US, Australia, Canada, and Europe began creating original
BJD sculpts, typically sculpting originals in clay, making silicone molds,
and casting in resin — either in-studio (artist cast) or via professional
casting companies (primarily in Asia). This is the category Glowmoon Doll
occupies. Western indie artists are valued for originality, artistic
distinctiveness, and direct collector relationships.

## § PART 2 — Anatomy and Terminology

### Construction
- **Material**: Polyurethane resin (hard, dense, paintable, can yellow with UV)
- **Stringing**: Elastic cord runs through hollow limbs and torso; S-hooks
  connect at joints
- **Joints**: Ball-and-socket at neck, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips,
  knees, ankles; double-jointing (peanut/cap joints) increases poseability
- **Finishing**: Parts are sanded, cleaned, and either sold blank or with a
  faceup applied

### Standard sizing (Volks-originated terms, used industry-wide)

| Term | Approx. height | Scale | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| SD / 1/3 | 58–70cm | ~1/3 human | Adult proportions; most common large size |
| MSD / 1/4 | 40–45cm | ~1/4 human | "Mini Super Dollfie" — child or slim adult |
| YoSD / 1/6 | 25–27cm | ~1/6 human | "Young SD" — child body |
| Tiny | 10–20cm | Varies | Wide range; often chibi or animal |

Sizing is not standardized across companies — measurements vary and
clothing/accessories do not automatically cross-fit.

### Customization vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Faceup** | All hand-painted facial features — eyebrows, lips, blush, eyelines; applied with acrylics/pastels, sealed with matte sealant |
| **Body blushing** | Painting the body for realism (knuckles, joints, shading) |
| **Eyes** | Glass, acrylic, or resin; removable via head-cap; sold separately |
| **Wig** | Removable; mohair, synthetic, or natural fiber |
| **Head-cap** | Removable top of head for eye/string access |
| **Faceplate** | Some dolls remove face rather than head-cap |
| **Blank** | Doll sold without faceup — for collector or artist to paint |
| **Full set** | Doll with faceup, eyes, wig, outfit, and accessories |
| **Restringing** | Replacing the elastic; required maintenance over time |
| **Sueding** | Adding suede or silicone discs inside joints to improve friction/posing |

## § PART 3 — Community Structure

### Online hubs
- **Den of Angels (DoA)** — Largest English-language BJD forum; 64,000+
  members. Invite-only access. Extensive wiki, buy/sell marketplace,
  and comparison threads. Strictly anti-recast; offenders are permanently
  banned. The authoritative community hub for serious collectors.
- **Castaways** — More relaxed policy on off-topic discussion and recast
  tolerance; popular alternative to DoA
- **Instagram** — Primary active community platform; #bjd, #abjd,
  #glowmoondoll and similar tags; doll photography, unboxing, styling
- **Tumblr (Dollblr)** — Active BJD subculture; faceup artists, OC content,
  community discussion
- **Discord** — Company-specific servers, faceup artist servers, regional
  collector groups
- **Facebook groups** — "Still Plays With Dolls: A BJD Group" and many others
- **YouTube** — Unboxing, faceup tutorials, vlogs, collection tours

### Conventions
- **Dolls Party** (Japan) — Volks-organized; flagship BJD convention
- **Dollectable** (San Francisco) — Long-running US convention
- **Resin Rose BJD Expo** (Clackamas, Oregon) — Pacific Northwest regional
  expo; directly relevant to Glowmoon's geographic market
- **Epic Doll Fair** — Online and in-person events
- Regional meetups organized through forums, Discord, and Instagram

### Community demographics
Adult collectors overwhelmingly. Skews toward people interested in character
creation, photography, costuming, and fine art. Historically female-skewing
but increasingly gender-diverse. Strong neurodivergent and LGBTQ+ presence.
Tight-knit with high brand loyalty; collectors research companies extensively
before purchasing.

## § PART 4 — Hobby Practices

### Doll photography
Photography is the primary medium for sharing BJD ownership. Direct lineage
from Bellmer's surrealist work through Japanese art doll photobooks to the
modern Instagram/Tumblr era. Styles range from simple documentation to
elaborate staged scenes with props, lighting, and post-processing. Technical
focus includes natural light, macro/portrait lenses, background styling,
and posing to show joint articulation naturally. Community etiquette expects
credit to sculptors and faceup artists in captions.

### OC / character building
A major driver of the hobby. Many collectors buy dolls specifically to
embody original characters (OCs) they have created — complete with name,
backstory, personality, and visual identity. "Shelling a character" (finding
the right sculpt for an OC) is a significant part of purchase motivation.
Dolls are renamed, given custom faceups to match the character's look, and
dressed accordingly. Rune from Glowmoon is a prime example of a sculpt
released with its own character lore — this is a major asset and direct
appeal to OC-driven collectors. Photo stories and roleplay using BJDs as
character vessels are common.

### Hybrid dolls
A hybrid is a doll assembled from parts sourced from different companies —
most commonly a head from one maker on a body from another. This is standard
and accepted practice, analogous to kitbashing in scale modeling. Key
challenges: resin color matching between companies, neck circumference
compatibility, and proportional harmony. Den of Angels has dedicated
comparison threads to assist. Collectors use hybrids to achieve specific
aesthetics or body types not available from a single company. For artist
dolls like Glowmoon, compatibility with popular bodies is a relevant
purchase consideration.

### Secondary market
Active resale ecosystem. Primary venues: Den of Angels Marketplace
(requires membership and post history), BJDCollectors.com, Etsy, eBay,
and social media. Key factors affecting resale value:
- **Rarity**: Limited editions command premiums, sometimes multiples of
  original retail. Discontinued sculpts from popular companies can reach
  $5,000+ on secondary market.
- **Condition**: Mint-in-box with original packaging holds premium; yellowed
  or damaged resin reduces value sharply
- **Faceup quality**: A skilled faceup adds significant value; amateur or
  damaged faceups reduce it
- **Customization**: Heavy mods (drilling, sanding) reduce value for most
  buyers; tasteful faceups and styling can increase it
- **Provenance**: Artist-direct purchases, certificates of authenticity, and
  documented history add trust and value for artist dolls specifically

## § PART 5 — The Recast Issue

### What recasting is
Recasting is the unauthorized reproduction of a sculptor's original work.
A recaster acquires a legitimate doll, creates silicone molds from it, and
casts copies in resin — selling them at a fraction of the original price.
The process takes approximately one day to mold and fifteen minutes to cast
a copy. It requires none of the months of sculpting, prototyping, or
mold-making investment the original artist made.

### Why it matters for original artists
BJD companies — including Glowmoon — are small operations. Most are run by
one to ten people. When recasters undercut original prices, they directly
reduce revenue to the artists and small teams who fund every future sculpt
from sales of current ones. Multiple companies have issued statements
describing the existential threat recasting poses to small studios. The
entire artist-doll market depends on collectors valuing original work.

### Community landscape
**Anti-recast position** (majority of the organized community): Recasting
is IP theft. Buying recasts funds criminal operations, harms artists directly,
and erodes the community that supports original work. Den of Angels enforces
a hard zero-tolerance policy — linking to a recast seller can result in
permanent ban. Faceup artists and accessory makers aligned with the
community generally refuse to work on recasts.

**Recast-friendly position** (minority, primarily Tumblr/informal spaces):
Price accessibility argument — legitimate dolls range from $300–$1,500+,
making the hobby exclusionary. Proponents argue large companies are less
harmed. This position is largely rejected by the organized community and
has no standing on major platforms.

**Glowmoon's position**: As an original artist, Glowmoon Doll is directly
harmed by recasting. Recast buyers are not the target market. The community
of collectors who support original artist work — the anti-recast majority
on DoA, Instagram, and convention circuits — is Glowmoon's core audience.

### Identifying recasts (for customer awareness)
- Unusually low price (well below company retail)
- Generic listing titles with no company name
- Multiple brands listed from one "seller"
- Photos stolen from company websites
- "Free faceup included" (recast faceups rarely match company quality)

## § PART 6 — Vinyl Dolls and Internal Frame Dolls

### Dollfie Dream (Volks, est. mid-2000s)
Developed concurrently with Super Dollfie. Vinyl shell over an ABS plastic
internal skeleton/armature. Anime-aesthetic; highly collectible. Licensed
character versions are a major product line (Sword Art Online, etc.). The
dominant Japanese vinyl doll. Community debate over whether IFDs (Internal
Frame Dolls) should be classified as BJDs — DoA considers them off-topic;
most collector spaces include them.

### Smart Doll (Danny Choo / Culture Japan)
60cm vinyl doll with internal frame; launched early 2010s. Founder Danny
Choo (son of designer Jimmy Choo) positioned Smart Doll around diversity
and inclusivity — notable for broader skin tone range and varied character
designs. Known for hand-applied faceups and premium packaging. Community
relationship is complex: strong following, but Danny Choo's direct responses
to criticism (including banning customers who publicly criticize the brand
on social media) have generated controversy. Technically more accessible
than resin BJDs (vinyl is more durable, no yellowing) but viewed by resin
purists as a separate category.

### Obitsu / other vinyl bodies
Affordable Japanese vinyl bodies (25–60cm) widely used as budget BJD bases
and hybrid bodies. Popular in the Dollfie Dream community and for customizers.

### Key vinyl vs. resin distinctions

| | Resin | Vinyl/IFD |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Polyurethane resin | PVC vinyl over ABS frame |
| Stringing | Elastic | Internal skeleton |
| Faceup | Acrylic/pastel; very paintable | More difficult to paint; can stain |
| Durability | Can chip/crack; yellows with UV | More impact-resistant; no UV yellowing |
| Aesthetic | Wide range; artist-controlled | Often anime-influenced |
| Community standing | "Classic" BJD | "IFD" — adjacent category |

## § PART 7 — Adjacent Collector Markets

### Sofubi (soft vinyl / 軟ビ)
Japanese soft vinyl toy tradition rooted in Kaiju (monster) figures from
the 1960s–70s. Modern sofubi is an indie art scene: small-run hand-cast
vinyl figures, often blank colorways sold for custom painting, or OOAK
(one-of-a-kind) hand-painted editions. Key figures: Maxtoy (Mark Nagata),
indie artists selling through galleries like Toy Art Gallery and Captivated.
Community overlap with BJDs is real — both involve hand-finishing, artist
relationships, and anti-recast ethics. Sofubi collectors appreciate limited
runs, artist provenance, and paintable blanks. Glowmoon's blank resin doll
model has conceptual overlap with the blank sofubi colorway model.

### Designer toys / urban vinyl
Broader collector category including BE@RBRICK (Medicom), Nendoroid and
Figma (Good Smile Company), Dunny (Kidrobot), and artist-designed limited
figures. Overlaps with streetwear/contemporary art world (KAWS collaborations,
etc.). Different audience from BJD — less customization-focused, more
display-and-collect. Price points range from $10 to $5,000+ for rare editions.
BE@RBRICK 1000% editions regularly sell at auction. Relevant to Glowmoon
only as broader context for the collectible figure market.

### Action figures
Highly articulated figures (McFarlane, NECA, S.H.Figuarts, etc.) occupy
adjacent space with BJDs in poseability and photography use. Collector
overlap exists for doll photographers drawn to dynamic posing. Generally
IP-licensed rather than original artist work. Production scale is mass-market
vs. BJD's small-batch or artist model.

### 3D printing (emerging influence)
SLA/MSLA resin printing increasingly used by BJD artists for prototyping and
accessories. Phrozen produces a BJD-specific resin. 3D-printed BJD parts
require significant post-processing (sanding, priming) to achieve the smooth
finish expected in the hobby. Community debate: 3D printing reduces barriers
to entry for artists but raises quality expectations and recast risk (a digital
file is easier to distribute than a physical mold). Net trend: accelerating
adoption for accessories and prototyping; slower for finished dolls.

## § PART 8 — Glowmoon in Context

### Market position
Glowmoon occupies the western indie artist BJD niche — original sculpts,
small-batch resin cast, hand-finished, sold direct. This is a premium,
trust-dependent category. Buyers are not purchasing a product; they are
commissioning a relationship with an artist.

### What collectors in this market value
- **Original character/lore** — Rune's backstory is a direct asset; collectors
  shell OCs and are drawn to dolls with existing narrative identity
- **Artist voice and backstory** — Isabel's identity as the sculptor matters;
  collectors want to know who made the doll
- **Blank availability** — Faceup artists and customizers specifically seek
  blank options; offering blank + faceup versions serves both audiences
- **Resin color range** — Skin tone variety is a growing community priority;
  offering diverse resin colors broadens appeal
- **Scale compatibility** — Noting which clothing/wig/eye sizes fit enables
  collectors to shop for their existing wardrobe
- **Photography-friendly posing** — Articulation quality matters for the
  photography use case; mention it
- **Anti-recast stance** — Stating it clearly signals belonging to the
  legitimate collector community; buyers who care about this look for the signal

### Red flags that erode trust in this market
- Vague or incomplete sizing/measurement information
- No clear artist identity behind the work
- Absence of care/maintenance guidance
- Positioning that feels mass-market rather than handmade/limited
- Not addressing recast policy at all (signals inexperience or indifference)
