# Comic Narrative Designer

## HOW TO USE THIS SKILL

**Arc and world level:** flexible. These are principles and frameworks — apply them selectively, not as a checklist to fill in mechanically.

**Episode level:** strict. The Episode Beat Sheet in Phase 3 defines a required structure. Every episode should hit every beat.

**Starting point:** If the user has a vague concept → start at Phase 1 (Concept Pressure Test). If they have characters but no plot → start at Phase 2 (Arc Design). If they have an arc but need episode structure → start at Phase 3.

---

## PHASE 1 — CONCEPT PRESSURE TEST

Before building anything, interrogate the concept. Weak concepts produce series that stall after 10 episodes.

**The five questions:**

1. **What does the protagonist WANT?** (External, conscious goal)
2. **What does the protagonist NEED?** (Internal, often unconscious — the thing they resist)
3. **Why are these in conflict?** (The gap between want and need IS the story)
4. **What is the worst thing that could happen to them?** (This should happen. Usually around the midpoint.)
5. **What would they have to give up or become to get what they need?** (This is the character arc endpoint)

**A concept is ready to develop when:** questions 1–3 have clear, distinct answers. If want and need are the same thing, there is no internal arc — the story will feel flat.

**The premise test:** Can you state the concept as: *"A [protagonist type] who [wants X but needs Y] must [face central conflict] or else [stakes]."* If not, keep working.

## PHASE 2 — ARC DESIGN (Flexible Framework)

### Season/Series Structure

Standard webtoon season: **20–60 episodes**. Plan in arcs of **3–12 episodes** each.

A season maps to STC's three-act structure:

| Season position | STC equivalent | Episode range (60-ep season) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening arc | Setup + Catalyst + Debate | Eps 1–10 |
| Rising action arc(s) | Break into 2 + Fun & Games | Eps 11–30 |
| Midpoint arc | Midpoint pivot + Bad Guys Close In | Eps 31–42 |
| Crisis arc | All is Lost + Dark Night | Eps 43–50 |
| Resolution arc | Break into 3 + Finale | Eps 51–60 |

For a **shorter first season (20–25 eps)**, compress the opening arc to 3–4 episodes and each subsequent arc to 4–5 episodes. The proportions matter more than absolute episode count.

### The 15 STC Beats — Adapted for Webtoon

Original percentages in parentheses; translate to your episode count by multiplying total episodes × %.

| # | Beat | % | Webtoon-specific note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening Image | 0–1% | First scroll: establish tone, protagonist, world in one visual sequence |
| 2 | Theme Stated | 5% | A line of dialogue or caption that hints at what the story is really about — reader won't recognize it yet |
| 3 | Set-Up | 1–10% | Introduce protagonist in their ordinary world. Establish what they want and what flaw stands in their way |
| 4 | Catalyst | 10% | The event that makes the old world impossible. Must happen by episode 3 at the latest in webtoon format |
| 5 | Debate | 10–20% | Protagonist resists the call. Show what they're risking by changing |
| 6 | Break Into Two | 20% | Protagonist makes an active choice. Story enters Act 2. In manhwa: this often aligns with a regression, isekai transfer, or accepting a power/mission |
| 7 | B Story | 22% | Introduce the relationship (romantic interest, mentor, rival) that will carry the theme |
| 8 | Fun & Games | 20–50% | "Promise of the premise" — deliver what the genre hook promised. Action: fights, power reveals. Romance: growing tension and near-misses. SoL: slice-of-life vignettes that deepen character |
| 9 | Midpoint | 50% | False victory or false defeat. Stakes shift. Often a revelation that reframes everything before it |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 50–75% | External pressure escalates; internal flaws get weaponized against the protagonist. Cracks form in allies and plans |
| 11 | All Is Lost | 75% | The protagonist's lowest point. Something or someone must die (physically or symbolically). Feel truly hopeless |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75–80% | Protagonist sits with the loss. The theme finally lands. This is the moment before transformation |
| 13 | Break Into Three | 80% | Synthesis: protagonist applies B-story wisdom to the A-story problem. New plan, new self |
| 14 | Finale | 80–99% | Confront the central conflict with the transformed protagonist. Subplots resolve |
| 15 | Final Image | 99–100% | Mirror or contrast of the Opening Image — shows how far the protagonist has traveled |

**Critical webtoon rule:** The Catalyst must land by episode 3. Manhwa readers drop series that fail to hook in the first episode and have not committed by episode 3. Do not spend 10 episodes on setup.

### Character Arc Design

Every major character needs an arc spine:

```
WANT: [External goal driving plot choices]
NEED: [Internal truth they're resisting]
WOUND: [Past event that created the false belief]
FALSE BELIEF: [What they think is true that isn't]
ARC ENDPOINT: [What they accept, give up, or become]
```

The B-Story character's function is to embody or model what the protagonist needs but can't see in themselves yet.

### The B Story / Relationship Arc

In manhwa — especially Romance/Drama — the B story IS the primary emotional engine. Map the relationship arc separately:

```
Stage 1 — Denial / Antagonism (eps 1–20%)
Stage 2 — Reluctant cooperation (20–40%)
Stage 3 — Growing attachment (40–60%)
Stage 4 — Threat / Separation / Betrayal (60–75%)
Stage 5 — Reconciliation / Confession (80–99%)
```

For Action/Fantasy: the B-story is more likely a mentor relationship or rival arc. For SoL: it's ensemble relationship deepening.

### A/B/C Storyline Threading

Borrowed from professional TV writers' rooms. Every episode can carry multiple simultaneous storylines at different emotional registers — this is what prevents tonal monotony in long-form serialized work.

**A Story** (~50–60% of episode): The primary conflict driving the season beat. Opens and closes the episode. Highest stakes.

**B Story** (~30–40% of episode): The thematic or emotional counterweight. Often the relationship arc. Should reflect or complicate the A story's theme — not run parallel without connecting.

**C Story / Runner** (~10–20%): Tonal relief or a subplot seed. Can be comedic relief in a drama, or a quiet character moment in an action series. Every C story should still connect to the episode's theme, even obliquely.

**Key rule:** If you removed a storyline and the episode felt no different, that storyline isn't doing its job. Each thread must earn its panel space.

**Webtoon application:** A/B threading is natural in most episodes. The C story is optional but powerful — it's where you plant seeds that pay off 15 episodes later, and it's where Slice of Life and ensemble casts breathe.

### The Hybrid Format (Serialized + Episodic)

The strongest webtoons use both structures simultaneously: **each episode is self-contained enough to re-hook a binge reader, while the season arc builds underneath**.

This mirrors prestige TV's best format: episodic in surface structure, serialized in character and theme.

- Each episode should answer one question it raised in the open
- The season arc question should advance but not resolve
- A new reader joining at episode 12 should feel oriented within the first 3 panels and invested by panel 15

**Accessibility principle:** The harder it is to join mid-series, the harder it is to grow your audience. Design every episode as if the reader just found it on a recommendation.

## PHASE 3 — EPISODE BEAT SHEET (Strict Template)

Every episode is a mini-arc. Apply this structure to every episode script.

```
EPISODE BEAT SHEET

Series: _______________
Episode #: ___   Title: _______________
Arc position: [opening / rising / midpoint / crisis / resolution]
Season beat this episode services: [STC beat name from Phase 2]

──────────────────────────────────────────
OPEN (first 5–10% of panels)
──────────────────────────────────────────
RE-ENTRY: Re-establish where we are without recapping.
  Drop into action or emotion — not explanation.
  [ Describe the opening image/scene: ]

EPISODE QUESTION: What question does this episode raise or continue?
  [ State it: ]

──────────────────────────────────────────
DEVELOPMENT (middle 60–70% of panels)
──────────────────────────────────────────
SCENE BEATS: List 2–4 beats that develop the episode question.
  Each beat should either escalate tension OR reveal character.
  Beat 1: 
  Beat 2: 
  Beat 3: 
  Beat 4 (if needed): 

MIDPOINT TURN: The episode must shift direction or raise stakes at ~50%.
  What changes here?
  [ Describe the turn: ]

──────────────────────────────────────────
CLOSE (final 10–15% of panels)
──────────────────────────────────────────
PAYOFF: Resolve (or partially resolve) the episode question.
  Note: resolution does NOT mean satisfaction — it can mean a worse situation.
  [ Describe the payoff: ]

HOOK: Final image or line that pulls the reader to the next episode.
  Choose a hook type:
  [ ] Cliffhanger (danger / unknown outcome)
  [ ] Emotional lure (unanswered feeling — "what will they do?")
  [ ] Revelation lure (partial reveal that demands explanation)
  [ ] Relationship shift (something changed between characters)
  
  [ Describe the hook: ]
```

**Pacing rule:** If the episode has no midpoint turn, it will feel flat. If the close has no hook, subscriber retention drops. These are not optional.

**Panel budget (manual production workflows):**
- Opening: 5–8 panels
- Development: 25–40 panels
- Close + Hook: 5–10 panels
- Total typical episode: 40–55 panels

*Note: integrated platforms like Dashtoon define episode length by scroll height and composition count rather than discrete panel numbers. The structural beats above still apply; the panel counts are a manual production heuristic only.*

## PHASE 3.5 — PACING ARCHITECTURE

Pacing operates at two independent scales. Both require deliberate design. Conflating them produces invisible problems.

### Macro Pacing (Season/Arc Level)
Controls how much story is allocated to each phase of the series. The most common macro failure: **front-loading** — all the exciting reveals in the first 30%, leaving filler to fill the rest.

**The fractal tension model:** Tension forms nested peaks at every scale. A season has a climactic peak. Each arc within the season has its own peak. Each episode has its own peak. Each panel sequence within an episode has its own peak. A story is fractals all the way down.

The practical implication: plan where your *major* reveals and confrontations occur across the full season before writing episode 1. If your map shows three big moments in the first 10 episodes and nothing substantial until the finale, redistribute.

**Mystery distribution rule:** Plant 3–5 significant unresolved questions in the first arc. Drop clues at regular intervals (every 4–6 episodes). Resolve at least one per arc to reward ongoing readers. Never let more than 8–10 episodes pass without a meaningful revelation.

**Macro rhythm pattern:** Escalating series of arcs, each raising stakes beyond the last, with deliberate breathing room arcs after high-intensity confrontations. Readers need recovery time before the next escalation hits.

### Micro Pacing (Episode/Panel Level)
Controls the reader's moment-to-moment scroll speed. The webtoon format makes micro pacing unusually controllable — you literally determine how many panels pass before the next beat.

**Tension/release is the unit:** Every micro-pacing decision is either building pressure or releasing it. Constant pressure exhausts readers within 10–15 panels. Constant release bores them.

- **Build tension:** Narrow panels, dense dialogue, tight gutters, fragmented action
- **Release tension:** Wide gutters, open panels with white space, reaction shots, silence
- **Peak moments:** Full-width panels with minimal or no text — let the image breathe
- A research finding worth knowing: readers who experience varied emotional swings report measurably higher immersion than those who experience consistent emotional tone. Deliberate peaks and valleys are not just structure — they're psychology.

**Dialogue economy:** Every speech bubble competes with the art for panel space. Each line should do at least two things: advance plot, reveal character, build tension, or drop a clue. Single-function dialogue slows micro pacing and wastes panels.

**Act-out technique (from TV):** Every major pacing checkpoint within an episode — not just the ending — should land on a mini-cliffhanger or unresolved beat. Think of each natural "break point" in the scroll (full-width panel, major gutter, scene change) as an act break that must earn the reader's continued scroll.

### Core Principle: The Iceberg

Build 8x more than you show. The world exists to inform the story; expository info-dumps are the primary failure mode in serialized comics. Reveal details through character action and dialogue, never through narrative pause.

**Rule of thumb:** If you feel the urge to stop the story and explain something, find a character who would naturally encounter or react to that thing instead.

### What to Build vs. What to Show

| Category | Build (for you) | Show (to readers) |
|---|---|---|
| History | Full timeline, key conflicts, who shaped the present | Only events that directly affect the current story |
| Power systems | Full rules, limits, costs | Glimpse the surface; reveal rules through consequence |
| Social structure | Full hierarchy, factions, relationships | What matters to your characters right now |
| Geography | Full map, climate, distances | Locations the characters inhabit |
| Culture | Beliefs, customs, values, taboos | Moments that reveal character through cultural lens |

### By Genre

**Action / Fantasy:**
- Define the power system first: What can it do? What is the cost or limit? What is it afraid of?
- Sanderson's First Law applies: A protagonist's ability to solve problems using magic or power is directly proportional to how well the reader understands that power.
- Build factions and their conflicting interests — these generate plot for free.
- The world's history should contain a wound (a past event whose consequences are still unresolved). That wound drives your main conflict.
- Iceberg is essential: tease the depth of history; don't explain it.

**Romance / Drama:**
- World = social environment + its rules and pressures on your characters.
- Define: What does this society reward? What does it punish? What does it shame?
- The central couple's obstacles should emerge from the world's rules, not coincidence.
- Emotional milieu matters more than geography: what does it feel like to be in this world?
- Contemporary: research the actual subculture (school systems, workplace dynamics, family structures) so cultural details land authentically.
- Historical/fantasy-romance: the social hierarchy and its limitations ARE the worldbuilding.

**Slice of Life / Comedy:**
- Build the ensemble: 4–6 recurring characters with distinct wants, fears, and roles in the group.
- Define the recurring location(s) — the café, school, shop, apartment — with enough detail that it feels inhabited.
- Establish running gags and character-specific behaviors that deepen with repetition.
- The world should feel like a place readers want to return to. Warmth and familiarity are the point.
- Slice of Life worldbuilding is accumulative — add texture episode by episode rather than front-loading it.

## PHASE 5 — GENRE BEAT CONVENTIONS

Each genre has structural expectations beyond STC. Violate them intentionally, not accidentally.

### Romance / Drama
- **Meet-cute or meet-conflict** must occur in episode 1. The chemistry (positive or antagonistic) must be legible immediately.
- The romantic pair must have a clear **obstacle rooted in character** (not external coincidence).
- Common manhwa structural moves: enemies-to-lovers, fake relationship, second-chance romance, regression arc where the protagonist knows the future of the relationship and must navigate differently.
- **Mandatory emotional beats:** first moment of genuine vulnerability; the betrayal or misunderstanding that separates the pair; the reconciliation.
- Readers binge-read romance. Payoffs must come at regular intervals (~every 5–8 episodes) or readers disengage.

### Action / Fantasy
- **Power reveal** should come in episode 1 or 2 — establish what the protagonist can do and what their ceiling is.
- Build toward **escalating antagonists**: each arc's villain should exceed the last's power level.
- **Training/growth arcs** are expected — the protagonist must visibly improve through effort.
- Common manhwa structural moves: system/status screens, dungeon delving, regression after death, OP protagonist with a hidden weakness or secret, tower-climbing structure.
- Each arc should culminate in a high-stakes confrontation with a clear winner. Ambiguous arc endings frustrate action readers.

### Slice of Life / Comedy
- **Episodic within a longer arc**: most episodes should work as standalone, but a background arc should develop beneath the surface.
- Establish the ensemble's **group dynamic and roles** (the chaos agent, the straight man, the earnest one, the secretly-competent one) by episode 3.
- Comedy relies on **setup and callback** across episodes — plant jokes and references early for payoff later.
- Emotional moments hit harder in SoL when they emerge without warning from a comedic or mundane scene. Don't telegraph them.
- Growth in SoL is subtle and cumulative — avoid dramatic transformation arcs that feel inconsistent with the tone.

## PHASE 6 — ANTI-PATTERNS

These are the most common narrative failures in webtoons. Check against them before finalizing any arc plan.

| Anti-pattern | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Late catalyst | Setup drags past episode 3 with no hook | Move catalyst to episode 1 or 2; fold setup into action |
| Flat protagonist | Character reacts to plot but never makes active choices | Every arc should have 1–2 beats where the protagonist drives events |
| Missing want/need gap | Protagonist wants what they need; no internal conflict | Identify the false belief; make them resist what would help them |
| Arc without payoff | Cliffhangers that don't resolve; mysteries that don't pay off | Map hooks and payoffs explicitly; track every planted question |
| Worldbuilding pause | Narrative stops so exposition can happen | Find a character to encounter or react to the information |
| Episodic drift | Episodes accumulate without building toward anything | Verify each episode services a named season beat |
| Inconsistent tone | Comedy episode followed immediately by heavy trauma without transition | Plan tonal trajectory; use transitional episodes |
| Villain without interiority | Antagonist exists only to create obstacles | Give antagonist a coherent want/need of their own |
| Front-loaded reveals | All big moments in first 30%; filler fills the rest | Map reveals across the full season before writing ep 1 |
| Constant tension | Every episode at maximum stakes — no breathing room | Insert deliberate recovery arcs after high-intensity confrontations |
| Mystery starvation | 10+ episodes pass with no revelation | Never go more than 8 episodes without resolving or advancing a planted question |
| On-the-nose dialogue | Characters state exactly what they feel ("I'm scared of losing you") | Replace with action, deflection, or behavior that implies the feeling |
| Single-function dialogue | Lines that only do one thing (advance plot OR reveal character, not both) | Audit every bubble — cut or rewrite lines doing only one job |
| Mid-entry hostility | New readers joining mid-series feel lost and don't stay | Design every episode so a new reader can orient in the first 3 panels |
| Plot pacing vs. page pacing confusion | Micro pacing is fine but the arc feels structurally lopsided | Audit separately: macro (where are your season's peaks?) and micro (panel rhythm within episodes) |
